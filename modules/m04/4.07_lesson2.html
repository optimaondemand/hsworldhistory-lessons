📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One year, three claimants, two great battles, and a kingdom permanently transformed. 1066 is the most consequential single year in English history.

The childless King Edward the Confessor died in January 1066. At least three men believed the English throne was rightfully theirs. Harold GodwinsonGOD-win-sunEngland's most powerful earl, crowned king within days of Edward's death, and dead within the year on the field at Hastings. Read more below. was England's most powerful earl, crowned almost immediately by the English council. Harald Hardrada, king of Norway, claimed an old inheritance agreement. William, Duke of NormandyWIL-yumThe Duke of Normandy whose claim, oath, and single day's battle at Hastings remade English landholding, language, and governance for centuries. Read more below., claimed Edward had personally promised him the throne years earlier.

William's claim rested on more than a promise. Around 1064, Harold himself had been shipwrecked on the Norman coast and fell into William's custody. According to Norman sources, Harold swore a solemn oath there, over sacred relics, to support William's claim to the English throne. Whether Harold made that oath freely, as William's chroniclers insisted, or under duress as a captive with little real choice, as English sources later suggested, is impossible to fully settle. What is certain is that Harold, once crowned king in England, broke whatever promise he had made. William took this as a personal betrayal, and it gave his invasion a moral case beyond simple ambition.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Harold Godwinson
GOD-win-sun

Harold had already proven himself a capable general years before 1066, campaigning successfully in Wales, and his swift coronation reflected a real English council consensus that he, not a foreign claimant, should hold the throne.

His campaign that autumn asked more of any army than most medieval commanders would risk: a forced march north to meet one invasion, a pitched battle won, and then an equally forced march south to meet a second invasion within roughly two weeks. Whether a few more days' rest at Hastings would have changed the outcome is exactly the kind of contingent question this course asks you to sit with, since Harold's own exhausted army was itself a direct product of his earlier victory.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
William the Conqueror
WIL-yum

William had already spent two decades securing his own duchy against rival Norman lords and an invasion by the King of France before he ever turned his ambitions toward England, and that hard-won experience commanding a fractious nobility shaped how ruthlessly he moved once he held the English crown.

The Harrying of the North shows the same man capable of both a genuinely systematic administrative achievement, the Domesday survey, and a deliberately brutal campaign of starvation against his own new subjects. This course asks you to hold both facts together rather than letting either one quietly explain the other away.

The battle itself lasted most of a single long day. Harold's forces held the high ground at Senlac Hill, forming the traditional Anglo-Saxon shield wallSHEELD wawlA dense Anglo-Saxon defensive formation of soldiers locking their shields together into a single barrier. See the glossary box., a dense barrier of interlocked shields that had held against cavalry charges before. William's Norman knights charged repeatedly and were repeatedly thrown back, until, according to several chronicles, the Normans staged a deliberate feigned retreat. Portions of the English line broke ranks to pursue what looked like a beaten enemy, and Norman cavalry turned and cut them down in the open. Harold was killed late in the fighting (according to legend, though not certainly, shot through the eye with an arrow) and his army broke. William was crowned king on Christmas Day, 1066, echoing, whether deliberately or not, Charlemagne's own coronation on the same feast day 266 years earlier.

Resistance to Norman rule did not end at Hastings. Rebellions continued in the north for several years. William's response, remembered as the Harrying of the North (1069-1070), was brutal. Entire villages were burned. Livestock and stored food were deliberately destroyed. The resulting famine was severe enough that chroniclers describe abandoned land and mass death across Yorkshire for years afterward. It is a hard episode to look at honestly, and this course asks you to hold it alongside William's genuine administrative achievements rather than let either one erase the other.

📚 PART 2 · DOMESDAY AND A NEW ARISTOCRACY

The Norman Conquest transformed English society from the top down. William replaced almost the entire Anglo-Saxon landholding aristocracy with Norman nobles, granted in exchange for their military service under a rigorous feudal land system imposed across the kingdom. He also built castles at key points across the realm. At first these were simple motte-and-bailey structures of earth and timber. Their purpose was to hold down a conquered population far larger than his own Norman garrison.

In 1086, William ordered the compilation of the Domesday BookThe Domesday BookA one-year survey that recorded nearly every landholding, plow, and pig in England, a scale of record-keeping no contemporary Western kingdom matched. Read more below.. It was an extraordinarily detailed survey of nearly every landholding, resource, and taxable asset in England. Its entries recorded even the number of plows and pigs on individual manors. Royal officials fanned out across every shire, asking the same set of questions of local juries under oath, and cross-checking the answers against earlier records. Nothing on this scale of central information-gathering existed anywhere else in contemporary Western Europe. It is, in its own way, a first-order political achievement in the Classical Republican sense: the capacity to know, tax, and administer an entire kingdom in systematic detail.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Domesday Book

Royal commissioners visited every shire and put the same standard set of questions to sworn local juries: who held the land under Edward the Confessor, who holds it now, how many plow teams and villagers work it, and what it is worth. Cross-checking answers against earlier tax records let the crown catch discrepancies rather than simply trust what it was told.

The survey's nickname came from ordinary English subjects, who compared its final, unappealable judgment on what anyone owned to the Last Judgment described in the Book of Life. No later English government attempted anything as comprehensive for centuries.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Eleanor of Aquitaine
EL-eh-nor

Eleanor's first marriage, to King Louis VII of France, was annulled after fifteen years and no surviving son, freeing her to bring Aquitaine to a second marriage instead. Within weeks of that annulment she married the much younger Henry, a decision that instantly made her new husband's holdings larger than his own king's.

Her later years were far less comfortable. Henry imprisoned her for over a decade after she backed their sons in rebellion against him, and only after his death did she re-emerge as a powerful dowager queen, helping govern England for her son Richard the Lionheart while he crusaded abroad.

📚 PART 3 · TOWARD THE ANGEVIN EMPIRE AND THE RULE OF LAW (CONTINUED)

Henry II's most lasting achievement, though, was legal. He expanded royal courts and standardized legal procedure across England. He sent royal justices on regular circuits to hear cases in the king's name, rather than leaving justice purely to local lords. He also encouraged the use of sworn local juries to establish facts in disputed cases. This created the beginnings of what is still called common lawKOM-un LAWLaw built up from accumulated royal court decisions and applied uniformly across a kingdom, rather than a patchwork of purely local custom. See the glossary box.. Common law is built up from accumulated royal court decisions and applied uniformly across the kingdom, rather than a patchwork of purely local custom. This built directly on Alfred's earlier law-making. It represents a real advance in the Classical Republican criterion of “administering common laws that regularly and predictably resolve conflicts of interest between citizens.”

Henry's expansion of royal justice did not go unchallenged. His court included scholars like John of Salisbury. His treatise on politics, the Policraticus, is what you will read today. It directly addresses the central question this era kept raising. What happens when a king's power grows too great? What distinguishes a lawful king from a tyrant? Henry's own reign would soon provide a dramatic test case. His former friend, Archbishop of Canterbury Thomas BecketBEK-itHenry II's own chancellor and closest friend, whose later resistance as archbishop ended in murder before his own altar. Read more below., clashed with him over how far royal courts could reach into church affairs. That conflict ended in Becket's murder in his own cathedral in 1170. The killing scandalized Christendom. It forced Henry into public penance at Becket's tomb. And it set real limits on how far a king could push the church.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Thomas Becket
BEK-it

Becket had served Henry loyally and lavishly as chancellor for years before his appointment to Canterbury, and Henry seems genuinely to have expected his old friend to keep favoring royal authority once he held the church's highest English office. Becket instead reversed himself completely, defending church privileges against the same king he had once served.

Four knights, apparently believing they were carrying out the king's wishes after an angry outburst from Henry, murdered Becket at the altar of his own cathedral. Henry always denied ordering the killing and performed public penance for it, but the damage to his authority over the church was done regardless of his intentions.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

England by 1170 combined three inheritances: French-speaking Norman nobility, Anglo-Saxon legal custom, and an increasingly assertive, systematized royal court. That combination was won partly through conquest and partly through a single fortunate marriage. This is the specific institutional mixture, W.2.18 asks you to notice, that eventually grows into England's distinctive constitutional and legal tradition.

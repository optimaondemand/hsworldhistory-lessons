📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A German monk nailed a list of academic debate points to a church door in 1517. He did not intend to split Western Christianity in two. He did anyway.

Criticism of the Roman Catholic Church's wealth, corruption, and doctrine did not begin with Martin Luther. A century and a half earlier, the English theologian John Wycliffe had criticized clerical wealth and sponsored an early English translation of the Bible. Wycliffe's followers, called Lollards, kept his ideas alive in England for decades after his death in 1384, despite repeated persecution. Wycliffe's English Bible, hand-copied since it predated the printing press, nonetheless circulated in several hundred manuscript copies, a remarkable reach for a banned text in the fourteenth century. The Christian humanist scholar Erasmus of Rotterdam, writing just before Luther, satirized clerical corruption sharply in works like In Praise of Folly, while remaining within the Catholic Church himself. Erasmus also published a scholarly edition of the Greek New Testament in 1516, a text Luther and other reformers used directly. A later saying held that “Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched.” Unlike Luther, Erasmus never broke from Rome. His own caution makes the contrast with Luther's later defiance especially sharp.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Jan Hus
yahn HOOS

Jan Hus was a Bohemian priest and reformer who carried criticisms of church wealth and doctrine, similar to England's John Wycliffe, into Central Europe a full century before Luther. Hus traveled to the Council of Constance in 1414 under an emperor's promise of safe conduct, expecting a fair hearing for his ideas.

The council condemned him regardless, and he was burned at the stake as a heretic in 1415, his safe conduct proving worthless once he arrived. His death helped touch off the Hussite Wars in Bohemia, a foretaste of the wider religious violence this module traces further in the century after Luther. The contrast with Luther's own later survival is instructive: when Luther faced a strikingly similar test at the Diet of Worms in 1521, his safe conduct held, and a protector willing to say no kept him alive long enough for his ideas to spread beyond any single ruler's reach.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Printing Press

The printing press, invented by Johannes Gutenberg around 1450, was the material and technological factor that let Luther's Ninety-Five Theses spread across the German lands within weeks, in a way no earlier reform movement's message ever could. Luther's Theses were originally written in Latin for academic debate; they were quickly translated into German and printed in the thousands.

Historians estimate that some 300,000 copies of Luther's various writings were in print by 1520, a scale of circulation that reformers like Wycliffe and Hus, working before the press existed, could never have approached. The press did not make Luther's arguments more correct than earlier reformers' similar criticisms. It made them impossible to contain in the way a single trial and execution had contained Hus a century before.

📚 PART 2 · A QUESTION OF TIMING

Rome did not take the threat seriously at first. According to a story that historians treat with some caution, Pope Leo X initially dismissed Luther's protest as a squabble among monks that would blow over on its own. Whatever the truth of that particular story, Rome's early response was slow and dismissive. By the time Church authorities moved to act, printed copies of Luther's arguments had already reached readers across the German lands. Contrast this with Jan Hus, whose movement Church authorities could still suppress a century earlier by trying and executing a single man in a single place. Luther's ideas, once printed and scattered across dozens of cities, had no single place left to suppress. Rome's response escalated in 1520, when Pope Leo X issued a formal bull, Exsurge Domineex-SUR-jeh DOM-ih-nayLatin for “Arise, O Lord,” Pope Leo X's 1520 bull threatening Luther with excommunication unless he recanted within sixty days. Luther's answer was to publicly burn the document instead, an act of open defiance that made any quiet resolution impossible.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

Luther did not set out to found a new church; he wanted to reform practices within the existing one through academic debate. Tomorrow's lesson shows how quickly that modest goal escaped his control. Weigh carefully what changed between Hus's era and Luther's. It was not that Luther's arguments were self-evidently better than Wycliffe's or Hus's; both forerunners had already made similar theological points. It was that printing, timing, and a protector's willingness to say no came together. Those three things let one reformer's protest survive long enough to become permanent, where two similar protests before him did not.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Baldwin I
BALD-win

Baldwin had already carved out his own County of Edessa in 1098, separate from the main crusader army, before he inherited Jerusalem on his brother Godfrey's death in 1100. Unlike Godfrey, Baldwin accepted the title king outright, judging correctly that a fragile new state needed a ruler with unambiguous royal authority, not a modest title.

His government's treatment of the kingdom's much larger Muslim, Jewish, and native Christian population was pragmatic rather than idealistic. Baldwin generally let conquered populations keep their property, laws, and religious practice, taxing them rather than expelling or forcibly converting them.

This was arithmetic more than tolerance for its own sake: a small Frankish ruling class had no realistic way to govern a mostly non-Frankish land except by leaving most of its people in place. Baldwin's pragmatic model shaped how the Crusader States governed themselves for the rest of their existence.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Military-Religious Orders

The Knights Templar, founded in 1119, and the older Knights Hospitaller filled a gap no ordinary feudal army could. Most crusaders who took the cross eventually went home. A permanent frontier state needed permanent soldiers instead.

Members of both orders took the same vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience as any monk, but trained and fought as heavily armored professional cavalry, garrisoning castles across the Crusader States year-round. Their combination of religious discipline and steady, standing readiness made them far more reliable than knights who served only a single campaigning season before returning to Europe.

Both orders grew wealthy and powerful in their own right, eventually running their own ships to move supplies, pilgrims, and reinforcements across the Mediterranean, as the vessels shown here suggest. That independence gave them influence rivaling the kingdom's own royal government, a tension that would outlast the Crusader States themselves by centuries.

📚 PART 1 · LAW ON A FRAGILE FRONTIER

A perpetually undermanned frontier society still needed some way to settle disputes among its own settlers. The Kingdom of Jerusalem eventually produced one: a written law code known as the Assizes of Jerusalemuh-SY-zezThe Crusader Kingdom's written law code, setting procedures for feudal courts, inheritance, and disputes between lords and vassals. See the glossary box.. It set out procedures for feudal courts, inheritance, and disputes between lords and vassals. It drew partly on French feudal custom the crusaders had brought with them. It drew partly, too, on plain practical necessity in an unfamiliar land. This course applies Classical Republican terms throughout. In those terms, the Assizes represent a genuine, if modest, attempt to administer common laws that regularly and predictably resolve conflicts of interest. It is one of the clearest first-order political achievements the fragile Crusader States managed at all.

Baldwin I himself set an early, pragmatic tone. His government did not treat the kingdom's much larger local Muslim, Jewish, and Eastern Christian populations as a permanent enemy to be expelled. It generally allowed them to keep their own property, laws, and religious practices, taxing them but rarely forcing conversion. This uneasy tolerance was not idealism. It was arithmetic: a small ruling population had no realistic way to govern a mostly non-Frankish land except by leaving most of its people in place.

📚 PART 2 · A FAILED CRUSADE, THEN SALADIN

The Crusader States' decline was not a straight line. The County of Edessa fell to Muslim forces in 1144. Alarmed European leaders responded by launching the Second Crusade (1147-1149), led by King Louis VII of France and Emperor Conrad III of Germany. It ended in near-total failure. The crusading armies were weakened by a difficult overland march through Anatolia. The failure shows that crusader fortunes did not simply decline in steady fashion after 1099; they suffered real, sudden reversals decades before Saladin ever appeared.

The Crusader States' fortunes depended heavily on Muslim disunity persisting. Zengi was the same warlord whose 1144 conquest of Edessa had triggered the Second Crusade in the first place. He and his son Nur ad-Din had already begun unifying Muslim Syria in the decades that followed. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Saladin
SAL-uh-din

Saladin rose through the service of Nur ad-Din, the Syrian ruler who had continued his father Zengi's work of uniting Muslim territory that had previously squabbled amongst itself as much as it fought the crusaders. Saladin took control of Egypt in the 1170s and, after Nur ad-Din's death, absorbed Syria as well, achieving the unified command his rivals had lacked.

At Hattin in 1187, Saladin let a brutally hot summer do much of his work for him, drawing the crusader army into a waterless position before destroying it in battle. Jerusalem's most sacred relic, a fragment believed to be the True Cross, was lost in the fighting and never recovered.

Saladin generally treated the surrendering population of Jerusalem afterward far more leniently than the crusaders had treated the city's own inhabitants in 1099, a contrast contemporaries on both sides noticed.

🛡 BATTLE
The Battle of Hattin, 1187

The crusader army marched to relieve the besieged town of Tiberias in brutal July heat, away from any reliable water source, a decision forced on a divided royal council against the advice of its more cautious members. Saladin's forces harried the column constantly and denied it access to water for two agonizing days before the final battle.

By the time the exhausted, dehydrated crusader force reached the twin hills known as the Horns of Hattin, it had little fight left. Saladin's army destroyed it almost completely, capturing the kingdom's own king and the relic believed to be the True Cross in the same battle.

With the kingdom's army gone, its cities fell to Saladin one after another within weeks. Jerusalem itself surrendered that autumn, ending eighty-eight years of Crusader rule over the city.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Alexios IV Angelos
uh-LEK-see-us AN-juh-lus

Alexios was the son of a Byzantine emperor who had been overthrown and blinded by his own brother. Exiled and desperate, Alexios approached the Fourth Crusade, then gathered in Venice for an attack on Egypt, with an offer: restore his father and himself to the throne, and he would pay the crusaders' enormous debts to their Venetian ship-financiers and reunite the Greek and Latin churches.

The crusaders agreed and restored him in 1203. Alexios then discovered his own treasury could not remotely cover what he had promised. Unpopular and unable to pay, he was overthrown and strangled by his own subjects within months of his restoration.

His death gave the crusaders camped outside the walls their final excuse. Rather than leave empty-handed, they attacked the city that had once been their whole reason for existing.

📚 PART 3 · THE FOURTH CRUSADE (CONTINUED)

The crusaders sacked the city with a thoroughness and brutality that shocked contemporaries on all sides. They looted relics, treasures, and even bronze statues. In the city's place, they installed a short-lived Latin Empire. This is a crucial piece of evidence for the causes of Byzantine decline you first encountered in Week 2. Alongside Justinian's Plague and centuries of external pressure, the Fourth Crusade shows fellow Christians dealing Byzantium one of the most devastating blows in its entire history. The Byzantines eventually recaptured Constantinople in 1261, but the empire never fully recovered its former strength. It is a genuinely unintended, deeply contingent consequence, with enormous long-term effects. It is also a sober reminder: a movement launched in the name of Christian solidarity could end by nearly destroying the very Christian empire it first set out to rescue.

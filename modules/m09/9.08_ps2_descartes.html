💡 BEFORE YOU READ

Published in 1641, Descartes's Meditations attempts to place all human knowledge on a completely certain foundation by first doubting everything that can possibly be doubted. This excerpt is from the famous Second Meditation.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
Meditations on First Philosophy, Meditation II (excerpted and lightly adapted)
Trans. John Veitch, 1901 (public domain). Excerpt.
“I suppose, accordingly, that all the things which I see are false… that I have no hands, no eyes, no flesh, no blood… What is there, then, that can be esteemed true? Perhaps this only, that there is absolutely nothing certain… But how do I know that there is not something different from all I have now enumerated, of which it is impossible to entertain the least doubt?… I must, in fine, consider that this proposition, I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind.”
📌 A NOTE FOR YOU

Descartes builds toward certainty not by observing the world more carefully, as Galileo and Bacon did, but by doubting the world entirely and finding one fact (his own thinking) that survives even total doubt. Two very different roads to the same era's confidence in human knowledge.

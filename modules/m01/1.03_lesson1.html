📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 410, a city that had not fallen to a foreign enemy in eight hundred years fell in three days. What actually broke, and when?

By the late fourth century, pressure was building on Rome's northern frontier. It had nothing to do with Roman decadence, and everything to do with events a thousand miles away. 👥 PEOPLES
The Goths
GOTHS

The Goths were a Germanic people farming and herding on the plains north of the lower Danube. They were not strangers to Rome. They had traded across the frontier for generations, many had served in Roman armies, and a good number were Christians, though of the ArianAIR-ee-un / AIR-ee-un-iz-umA Christian teaching, judged heresy by the mainstream church, holding that Christ the Son was created by, and ranks below, God the Father. See the glossary box. kind that most Romans regarded as heresyHAIR-uh-seeA belief that a religious authority has officially judged to contradict accepted teaching. See the glossary box.. What arrived in 376 was not a war band but a population on the move: fighting men with their wives, children, wagons and cattle, pushed west by the Huns.

That distinction matters for the whole lesson. A raiding party can be driven off. A people who have crossed a river with everything they own cannot go back, and must be settled somewhere. Rome never managed to do it.

🛡 BATTLE
Adrianople, 378
ay-dree-uh-NOH-pul

Valens attacked on a hot August afternoon without waiting for his western colleague's army to arrive. His men reached the Gothic wagon circle tired and thirsty. While the infantry was pressing forward, the Gothic cavalry returned from foraging and struck the Roman flank, and the Roman line was crushed together so tightly that soldiers could not raise their swords.

Two thirds of the eastern field army died, and Valens died with them. Scholarship does not support a confident battlefield plan, since the ancient accounts disagree about where the wagons stood. What is certain is the shock. A Roman army had been beaten in open battle inside the empire, and everyone on both sides knew it.

📚 PART 1 · A FRAGILE PEACE

Adrianopleay-dree-uh-NOH-pulThe battle in which the Goths destroyed an eastern Roman army and killed the emperor Valens. Read more below. did not end Rome. It began three decades of uneasy coexistence. The Goths, under a young leader named AlaricAL-uh-rikThe Gothic commander who spent thirty years seeking a legal place inside the empire and ended by sacking its oldest city. Read more below., were never fully absorbed and never fully expelled. Alaric served Rome as a general more than once, most notably leading Gothic troops for the Eastern emperor Theodosius I at the bloody Battle of the Frigidus in 394. When Theodosius died the next year, Alaric felt that sacrifice had bought his people nothing. The Goths rose again under his own leadership. For over a decade afterward, his central demand was not land or plunder but an office. He wanted formal Roman appointment as a magister militummah-JIS-ter MIL-ih-tumLatin for “master of soldiers,” the top Roman military command Alaric spent years trying to be granted. Roman courts, both East and West, repeatedly dangled this office and withdrew it. They used his army as a threat against each other while refusing to make him a genuine partner in either government.

Contingency, not inevitability, drove what happened next. Suspicion hardened at court, and in August 408 Stilicho was executed on Honorius's orders. His death triggered a second disaster. Roman soldiers in Italy turned on the families of the Gothic troops who had served under him. Roughly thirty thousand Goths, their families murdered by the state they had served, defected on the spot to Alaric.

With Stilicho gone and his own army suddenly doubled, Alaric marched on Rome three times in three years. In 408 he lifted his first siege only after a huge ransom. In 409 he installed a puppet emperor when talks with Honorius collapsed again. In 410 he tried a third time, after that puppet proved useless and negotiations broke down a final time. On 24 August, 410, after this third siege, a gate was opened from within the city, and the Visigoths entered.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Alaric
AL-uh-rik

Alaric was born around 370, probably on an island in the Danube delta, and grew up in the unsettled years after his people crossed into the empire. He first appears in the record as a Roman officer, leading Gothic troops for the emperor Theodosius at the Frigidus in 394. He watched those troops take terrible casualties and receive nothing for it.

What he wanted afterwards was not plunder but an appointment. He asked, repeatedly, to be made a Roman general with a district to feed his people, and both imperial courts kept offering the post and taking it back. He was still negotiating in 410 when the talks failed for the last time. He died of fever within months of taking Rome, aged about forty, with his people no closer to the settled home he had spent his life demanding.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Stilicho
STIL-ih-koh

Stilicho's father was a Vandal officer in Roman service and his mother a Roman. That mixed background was ordinary in the late Roman army and fatal in late Roman politics. Theodosius trusted him enough to marry him to his own niece and to leave him guarding his son Honorius, which made Stilicho the effective ruler of the West from 395.

He beat Alaric twice and then paid him, judging that a Gothic army bought was cheaper than a Gothic army fought. After the Rhine frontier broke in 406, that judgement looked to his enemies like collusion with the enemy. Honorius had him arrested and beheaded in August 408. Stilicho refused to let his own guards resist, and went to the executioner without a fight.

📚 PART 2 · A SACK UNLIKE THE OTHERS

What followed shocked the ancient world less for its violence than for the fact that it happened at all. Rome had not been taken by a foreign army since the Gauls under Brennus, some eight hundred years earlier. Saint Jerome was working on his translation of Scripture in Bethlehem. He wrote that his voice failed him with sobs when the news reached him: “The City which had taken the whole world was itself taken.” Refugees fleeing the sack scattered across the Mediterraneanmed-ih-tuh-RAY-nee-unThe inland sea the Romans called mare nostrum, meaning our sea. Yet by the standards of ancient siege warfare, Alaric's sack was restrained. Alaric was himself a Christian, an ArianAIR-ee-un / AIR-ee-un-iz-umA Christian teaching, judged heresy by the mainstream church, holding that Christ the Son was created by, and ranks below, God the Father. See the glossary box. Christian, of a different theological stripe than most Romans, but a Christian nonetheless. He ordered that anyone who took refuge in the city's churches, particularly Saint Peter's Basilica, be spared and their property left untouched. Looting and burning did occur, and the psychological blow to Roman confidence was immense. But the massacre some feared did not fully materialize. This restraint is not a minor detail. It is the reason Augustine of Hippo, writing a few years later, could argue that Rome's own God, not her legions, had shown her more mercy than she deserved. You will read his argument directly tomorrow.

That argument mattered. Many Romans, still loyal to the old gods, blamed the sack on Rome's abandonment of pagan worship. They claimed the city's true protectors had withdrawn their favor once the temples closed. Augustine's City of God, the primary source you will read tomorrow, was written directly against that claim, using historical evidence about Rome's own past disasters rather than theology alone. Even people living through the sack itself were already arguing about why Rome fell when it did.

Gothic tradition holds that his followers diverted the river Busento. They buried him in its bed with treasure taken from Rome, then let the water flow back so no one could find the grave. His personal authority had held his army together, and his sudden death left the Goths without a clear plan.

Leadership passed to his brother-in-law Athaulf. In 414 he married Galla PlacidiaGAL-uh pluh-SID-ee-uhAn emperor's sister, taken captive in the sack of Rome, who married her captors' king and later governed the West herself. Read more below., Honorius's own sister, taken captive during the sack. According to the Roman historian Orosius, Athaulf said he had once dreamed of replacing Rome with a Gothic empire. But he had come to see the Goths lacked the law and order to govern one, so he chose instead to use Gothic strength to restore Roman institutions. Sincere or not, the marriage was real. It points toward a pattern you will see again this quarter: barbarian kings settling not to destroy Rome, but to inherit it.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Galla Placidia
GAL-uh pluh-SID-ee-uh

Galla Placidia was the daughter of Theodosius I and half-sister of the emperor Honorius. She was in Rome when the city fell in 410 and left it as a hostage of the Goths, travelling with them for four years through Italy and southern Gaul.

In 414 she married Athaulf at Narbonne, in a Roman ceremony, with a Gothic king wearing a Roman cloak. After his murder she was returned to her brother, remarried to a Roman general, and bore the future emperor Valentinian III. When Honorius died she secured the throne for her six-year-old son and ruled the West as regent for twelve years. Her career is a useful corrective. The line between Roman and barbarian in this period was crossed by marriage at least as often as by war.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

It is tempting to read 410 as the moment "Rome fell." It was not. The Western imperial government would sputter on for another 66 years, and the Eastern half would outlast it by a thousand. But 410 is the moment the illusion of Roman invulnerability broke. It shows you the kind of causation this course asks you to look for: not a grand law of historical decline, but particular men (Valens, Alaric, Stilicho, Honorius, Athaulf) making particular decisions, any one of which could plausibly have gone differently. A refused office, a court intrigue, a massacre of soldiers' families, a king's fever, a captive princess's marriage. Each link in this chain was a human choice or a human accident, not the working-out of some larger law of decline. Keep that habit of mind. You will need it every week this quarter.

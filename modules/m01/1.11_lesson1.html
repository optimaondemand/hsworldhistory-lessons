👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Constantine the Great
KON-stan-teen

Constantine was proclaimed emperor by his father's troops in Britain in 306 and spent eighteen years fighting rivals before he ruled alone. The marble head beside this box comes from a seated statue perhaps twelve metres tall. The eyes are deliberately oversized and turned upward, which is how his own age wanted him seen: not a first citizen, but something closer to a sacred monarch.

Three of his decisions outlived him by a thousand years. He legalised Christianity in 313, he called and chaired the Council of Nicaea in 325, and he founded a new capital in 330. Historians still argue about how far he was a convert and how far a calculator. The honest answer is that the evidence supports both, and that a ruler's motives do not have to be pure for his choices to reshape the world.

📚 PART 1 · A CHRISTIAN EMPIRE

Constantine's other transformation was religious. In 313, with his co-emperor Licinius, he issued the Edict of Milan, legalizing Christianity throughout the empire after centuries of intermittent persecution. Constantine himself was baptized only on his deathbed in 337. Historians still debate how much of his own faith was genuine conviction and how much was shrewd politics, recognizing Christianity's spreading influence rather than merely deciding to adopt it. Both readings can be true at once. A Great Man does not need pure motives to change history. In 325, Constantine went further still. He personally summoned bishops from across the empire to the Council of Nicaeany-SEE-uhThe town across the water from the capital where Constantine summoned the bishops in 325 to settle the dispute over the nature of Christ. Find it on the map. to settle a bitter dispute over the nature of Christ, the same Arian controversy you will meet again through the Goths and the Vandals. An emperor calling and presiding over a council of churchmen was itself something new. It was a fusion of political and religious authority that would remain a hallmark of the Byzantine state long after Constantine was gone. By 380, under Emperor Theodosius Ithee-uh-DOH-shusThe last emperor to rule both halves of the Roman world, and the ruler who made Christianity the empire's official religion. Read more below., Christianity became not merely legal but the empire's official religion, and the old pagan temples began to close.

In 395, Theodosius divided administration of the empire between his two young sons. Arcadius ruled the East from Constantinople, and Honorius ruled the West from Italy. This was meant as an administrative convenience, not a permanent split, but it became one. The West, as you saw last week, collapsed within a century under Honorius and his successors. The East, under Arcadius's line, did not.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Theodosius I
thee-uh-DOH-shus

Theodosius was a Spanish general's son, called out of retirement in 379 to repair the disaster at Adrianople. He could not destroy the Goths, so he settled them inside the empire as allies under their own leaders, a bargain that shaped everything you read about last week.

In 380 he made Christianity the official religion of the state, and the old temples began to close. He also submitted to public penance when Bishop Ambrose refused him communion after a massacre at Thessalonica, which set a long precedent about whether an emperor stands above the church or inside it. The silver dish beside this box was made for his reign and shows him enthroned, larger than the men around him, handing out an office. On his death in 395 he left the East to one son and the West to the other.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Theodosian Walls
thee-uh-DOH-shun

The walls were begun under Theodosius II in 413 and finished in their mature form after an earthquake in 447. They run about six and a half kilometres from the Golden Horn to the Sea of Marmara, and they work in depth rather than in height. An attacker first met a broad ditch that could be flooded, then a low outer wall with its own towers, then a terrace, then the main wall standing about twelve metres high with ninety-six towers along it.

The masonry is the clever part. Bands of brick run through the limestone every few courses, tying the wall together and letting it flex in an earthquake instead of shearing. That is why so much of it is still standing today, and why it turned back Avars, Arabs, Bulgars and Rus for eight centuries.

📚 PART 3 · CONTINUITY AND DEPARTURE

They called themselves Rhomaioiroh-MY-oyGreek for “Romans,” what the people of the Byzantine Empire called themselves for the whole of its existence. See the glossary box., Romans, until the empire's final fall in 1453. The name “Byzantine” is a label historians invented centuries later, after the empire was gone. It was meant to distinguish the later, Greek-speaking Christian state from the pagan, Latin-speaking Rome of the Republic and early Empire. That labeling choice matters. It can make an unbroken fourteen-century state feel, to a modern reader, like something separate from “real” Rome, when the people living through it never made that distinction at all.

In important ways, the ByzantinesBIZ-un-teenThe modern historians' name for the Eastern Roman Empire; the people who lived in it always called themselves Romans, never “Byzantines.” See the glossary box. were right to call themselves Romans. Byzantine emperors kept Roman law, Roman administrative titles, and, for a long time, a functioning Senate. Roman roads, Roman taxation, and a professional, salaried army continued largely intact in the East for centuries after they had broken down in the West.

Yet real departures were underway. Latin gradually gave way to Greek as the empire's working language. The emperor's role fused political and religious authority far more tightly than in pagan Rome, with the emperor understood as God's regent on Earth. And the empire's center of gravity, culturally and economically, shifted permanently toward the Greek-speaking eastern Mediterranean. Whether you call this state “Rome continued” or “something new grown from Rome's roots” is a real historical judgment, not a settled fact. It is one you will be asked to defend yourself.

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
On Christmas Day, 800, a Frankish king knelt in Saint Peter's Basilica and rose an emperor. Was this the revival of Rome, or something new wearing Rome's name?

You met the Carolingian family last week as Mayors of the Palace, the officials who had quietly out-governed the Merovingian kings they nominally served. In 751, Charlemagne's father, Pepin the Short, formalized the takeover: with papal blessing, he deposed the last Merovingian king and took the crown himself, founding the Carolingian dynasty. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Charlemagne
SHAR-luh-mayn

Charlemagne stood unusually tall for his time, by Einhard's account, and remained physically active into old age, hunting and swimming even as an old man. He could not read fluently and never mastered writing well himself, a fact that makes his sponsorship of the Carolingian Renaissance, which you will study tomorrow, more surprising rather than less.

The portrait beside this box is a posthumous idealized image, painted by Durer roughly seven centuries after Charlemagne's death, since no reliable contemporary likeness survives. What is not idealized is the record of his campaigning: over four decades he fought almost every year of his adult life, and the empire he left behind in 814 was the largest united under one ruler in Western Europe since Rome itself.

📚 PART 1 · A LIFETIME OF CONQUEST

Charlemagne did not start out as sole ruler. When Pepin the Short died in 768, Frankish custom split the kingdom between his two sons, Charlemagne and his younger brother Carloman. The brothers ruled uneasily side by side for three years, cooperating only when they had to. In 771, Carloman died suddenly, still in his twenties. Charlemagne moved quickly to secure sole rule over the whole kingdom before Carloman's young sons or their mother could organize a rival claim. Had Carloman lived into old age, Frankish history might have run through two smaller, rival kingdoms rather than one growing empire. It is exactly the kind of turning point this course asks you to notice: a matter of one brother's lifespan, not some larger law of history.

The Lombard kingdom of Italy came first. King Desiderius of the Lombards, whose daughter Charlemagne had married and then set aside, sheltered Carloman's widow and sons, a direct threat to Charlemagne's throne. In 773, Charlemagne invaded Italy and besieged the Lombard capital of PaviaPAH-vee-uhThe Lombard capital Charlemagne besieged through the winter of 773 to 774. Its fall ended independent Lombard rule in Italy. Find it on the map. through a long winter, taking the city the following year. He deposed Desiderius and took the Lombards' own Iron Crown for himself, ruling northern Italy directly as Frankish king. This gave Charlemagne a base in Italy from which to deal directly with the pope, a relationship that would matter enormously by the year 800.

Over more than four decades of nearly continuous campaigning, Charlemagne also pushed the frontier of Frankish rule into northern Spain. In a brutal three-decade war, he also subdued and forcibly Christianized the pagan Saxons of northern Germany. By the 790s, Charlemagne ruled the largest territory united under one government in Western Europe since the fall of Rome. That territory included modern France, the Low Countries, much of Germany and Italy, and parts of Spain.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Widukind
VEE-doo-kint

Widukind never commanded a unified Saxon state; Saxony in his day was a patchwork of tribal groups without a single king. What he offered instead was a pattern other resistance leaders could follow: strike while Frankish armies were occupied elsewhere, then retreat into Denmark once they turned back to face him.

The painting beside this box shows the moment tradition remembers best: Widukind kneeling before Charlemagne at Paderborn in 785 to accept baptism, with Charlemagne reportedly standing as his godfather. Whether that reconciliation was sincere on both sides or a practical peace between exhausted enemies, it removed Saxon resistance's most capable leader and let the long Saxon war finally wind down.

🛡 BATTLE
Roncevaux Pass, 778
ron-suh-VOH

Charlemagne's Spanish campaign of 778 ended in failure, and as his army withdrew north across the Pyrenees, Basque forces ambushed its rear guard in the pass at Roncevaux. Frankish sources treat the episode as a minor disaster: a rear guard destroyed, several officers killed, including one recorded only as Hruodlandus, prefect of the Breton march.

That obscure officer is the same Roland whose death became the centerpiece of the Song of Roland, the epic you will read as a primary source next week. The poem swaps the historical Basque ambushers for a vast Muslim army and turns a forgettable defeat into a tale of martyrdom and betrayal. The gap between the recorded event and the legend it produced is itself a lesson in how medieval memory worked.

See the glossary box., Emperor of the Romans. According to Einhard, Charlemagne's own biographer, whom you will read tomorrow, Charlemagne claimed he would not have entered the church that day had he known the pope's intention.

Was Einhard's claim true, or a piece of careful reputation management after the fact? We cannot know for certain: another example of genuine historical uncertainty you should sit with rather than resolve too quickly. What is certain is the political logic behind the coronation. The Byzantine throne was at that moment held by Empress Irene. Many in the West, rightly or wrongly, used this to argue the imperial throne was effectively vacant, since, in their view, a woman could not rightfully hold it. Leo's coronation of Charlemagne asserted, in effect, that the true seat of Roman imperial authority in the West had returned to a Frankish, Latin-speaking, and papally-sponsored throne. The Byzantines never accepted this. They considered it outright usurpation of a title that, in their eyes, still belonged to Constantinople alone.

Charlemagne had already been building a capital worthy of an emperor before the title itself arrived. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Pope Leo III
LEE-oh

Leo owed his position to Charlemagne twice over. In 799 a hostile Roman faction assaulted him and tried to force him from office; he fled north to Charlemagne, who personally saw him restored to Rome. The debt between them ran the other way a year later, when Leo settled a live dispute about the imperial title by placing a crown on Charlemagne's own head.

The illumination beside this box shows that coronation. Whether Leo acted on his own initiative or with Charlemagne's quiet foreknowledge is genuinely uncertain, and Einhard's own account hints both ways. What is certain is the claim the ceremony made: that Roman imperial authority in the West now ran through a Frankish, papally sponsored throne, a claim Constantinople never accepted.

🏯 CITY
Aachen
AH-ken

Aachen had no Roman imperial pedigree; Charlemagne chose it for its central location and its warm springs, not for any inherited prestige. He made up for that lack of pedigree by building deliberately: a palace complex crowned by a domed chapel modeled closely on the great church of San Vitale in Ravenna, itself a Byzantine imperial commission two centuries earlier.

The photograph beside this box shows that chapel's interior as it survives today, one of the few Carolingian buildings still standing largely intact. Importing marble columns and mosaic craftsmen from Italy to a site deep in Frankish territory was itself a political statement: this new Rome in the north intended to look like an empire's capital, not merely claim the title on paper.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

Whatever Charlemagne's private feelings, the coronation of 800 created a lasting Western institution. It was the idea of a Holy Roman EmperorHOH-lee ROH-mun EM-per-erThe Western imperial title and institution created by Charlemagne's coronation, crowned by the pope, meant to carry on Rome's authority in the West. See the glossary box., crowned by the Pope, as the secular protector of Christendom. That institution did not automatically pass smoothly to the next generation. Charlemagne spent his final years arranging for his surviving son, Louis the Pious, to be crowned co-emperor in 813. He hoped to avoid exactly the kind of succession chaos that had briefly divided the kingdom after Pepin's death. You will see the Holy Roman imperial title, and its recurring tensions with the papacy, return sharply in Week 8.

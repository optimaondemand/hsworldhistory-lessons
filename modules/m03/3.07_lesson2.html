📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Charlemagne could not read fluently and never learned to write well. He nonetheless launched the most significant revival of learning Western Europe had seen since Rome's fall.

By the late 8th century, literacy and classical learning had decayed badly across the former Western Empire. Even many clergy struggled with correct Latin. Charlemagne was aware of this decline. He was determined to have an educated clergy capable of properly administering sacraments and interpreting Scripture. 📚 PART 1 · A DELIBERATE REVIVAL

In 789, Charlemagne issued the Admonitio Generalisad-moh-NEE-tee-oh jen-er-AH-lisLatin for “General Admonition,” Charlemagne's 789 decree ordering every monastery and cathedral to run a school and correct corrupted religious texts. See the glossary box. (“General Admonition”), a sweeping capitularykuh-PICH-oo-lair-eeA Frankish royal decree, organized into numbered chapters. See the glossary box., or royal decree. Among other reforms, it ordered every monastery and cathedral to maintain a school for training clergy. It also instructed monks to correct and standardize the badly corrupted texts of Scripture and the church fathers. These errors had accumulated through centuries of careless copying. This was not learning for its own sake in some abstract sense. It was learning in direct service of good government and right worship. That is further evidence, in the spirit of this course's approach to history, that ideas and institutions reinforce each other. Neither one simply causes the other. The document's eighty-two chapters ranged widely, from clergy conduct to the calendar. It even demanded that every priest be able to calculate the correct date of Easter each year. This was a genuinely difficult task requiring real skill in arithmetic and astronomy, exactly the kind of quadriviumTRIV-ee-um / kwad-RIV-ee-umLatin names for the two halves of the classical curriculum: the trivium (grammar, rhetoric, logic) and the quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy). The practical results were remarkable. Carolingian scribes developed a new, cleaner, more legible handwriting style called Carolingian minusculekair-oh-LIN-jee-un MIN-us-kyoolThe clear, legible handwriting reform that is the direct ancestor of the lowercase letters printed on this page. Monastic scriptoriaskrip-TOR-ee-umLatin for a monastery's manuscript-copying workshop. See the glossary box. across the empire began systematically copying classical Latin texts. A very large share of the ancient Roman literature that survives today exists only because Carolingian monks copied it in the 8th and 9th centuries. Copying a single complete Bible by hand could take a monk the better part of a year, working carefully by candlelight or window light. Every letter mattered. A miscopied word could distort a passage of Scripture for every reader who copied it afterward. Without this deliberate, painstaking act of preservation, much of what we know of Roman literature would simply be lost. That includes works of Cicero and Livy that Carolingian copies alone kept from vanishing.

The revival reached beyond words on a page. Carolingian workshops produced richly illuminated manuscripts, hand-painted with gold leaf, vivid pigments, and detailed portraits of saints and evangelists. The manuscript page beside this box, a Bible in the recension Alcuin himself supervised around 840, shows the script doing exactly the job it was designed for: page after page of readable, standardized text. Because Carolingian monks used this script to copy classical Latin authors as well as Scripture, a very large share of the Roman literature that survives today survives specifically in this handwriting.

📚 PART 2 · SYNTHESIS, NOT SUBSTITUTION

The Carolingian Renaissance is a clear, compact example of the larger thesis this course has been building since Week 1. Western civilization arose from a genuine synthesis of Greco-Roman learning, Judeo-Christian faith, and the political and social structures of Germanic peoples like the Franks. Alcuin's classroom curriculum, the triviumTRIV-ee-um / kwad-RIV-ee-umLatin names for the two halves of the classical curriculum: the trivium (grammar, rhetoric, logic) and the quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy). See the glossary box. and quadrivium (grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy), was inherited directly from Roman education. The purpose of that curriculum was overwhelmingly religious, training clergy and administrators for a Christian kingdom. And the political structure funding and enforcing it all was distinctly Frankish and Germanic, not Roman in origin. Royal capitularieskuh-PICH-oo-lair-eeA Frankish royal decree, organized into numbered chapters. See the glossary box., and a warrior aristocracy granted land in exchange for service, were both Frankish tools, not inherited Roman ones.

The cathedral and monastery schools the Admonitio Generalisad-moh-NEE-tee-oh jen-er-AH-lisLatin for “General Admonition,” Charlemagne's 789 decree ordering every monastery and cathedral to run a school and correct corrupted religious texts. See the glossary box. required did not disappear once Charlemagne's empire itself began to weaken. Many kept operating for centuries afterward, training generation after generation of clergy in the same basic curriculum Alcuin had assembled. By the High Middle Ages, several of the strongest cathedral schools in cities like Paris grew directly into the first universities of Western Europe. You will study this development in Week 11. The Carolingian Renaissance did not create the medieval university on its own. But it kept the institutional habit of organized, text-based schooling alive long enough for that later development to build on it. A student walking into a 12th-century cathedral school and one walking into an 8th-century Carolingian monastery school would have recognized much of the same basic method. Both relied on a master, a fixed body of texts, and the triviumTRIV-ee-um / kwad-RIV-ee-umLatin names for the two halves of the classical curriculum: the trivium (grammar, rhetoric, logic) and the quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy). 💪 WHY IT MATTERS

None of these three elements alone produced the Carolingian Renaissance. Roman learning without Christian purpose would have had no institutional home to survive in. Christian purpose without Roman learning would have had no curriculum to teach. And neither would have reached across an empire this size without Frankish royal power funding schools and enforcing capitularieskuh-PICH-oo-lair-eeA Frankish royal decree, organized into numbered chapters. See the glossary box.. It took all three together. Keep this synthesis in mind. It is the same formula, differently mixed, that you will see recur across medieval and early modern Western Europe for the rest of this course.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Mehmet II
meh-MET

Mehmet II became Ottoman sultan for the second time in 1451, at only nineteen years old, determined to succeed where every previous besieger of Constantinople had failed. He commissioned the Hungarian engineer Orban to cast some of the largest cannon ever built, after the Byzantines themselves could not afford Orban's price. He ordered dozens of ships hauled overland on greased logs to bypass the great chain sealing the Golden Horn, a feat of engineering audacity that stunned attackers and defenders alike.

After the city fell on 29 May 1453, Mehmet allowed his soldiers the traditional three days of plunder, then halted the sack himself and began rebuilding the city he intended to make his own capital. He resettled merchants and craftsmen from across his empire, repaired its markets and aqueducts, and within a generation restored Constantinople to a thriving imperial capital. He took the additional title “Caesar of Rome” for himself, understanding exactly what his conquest, and his careful rebuilding of it, had accomplished.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
Orban's Cannon
OR-bahn

Orban first offered his cannon-casting skill to Constantine XI, who wanted the weapon badly but could not meet Orban's price after decades of Byzantine financial decline. Orban then went to Mehmet II, who paid whatever he asked. The result was a bronze bombard reportedly some twenty-seven feet long, able to hurl a stone ball weighing over half a ton.

It took sixty oxen and hundreds of men to haul it into firing position outside the walls, and the gun could manage only a handful of shots a day before it needed to cool. Even so, sustained bombardment through weeks of siege gradually shattered stonework that had turned back every attacker for a thousand years. The Dardanelles Gun pictured here, cast a decade later on the same design, shows the scale of the technology that made the 1453 siege different from every siege before it.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Constantine XI Palaiologos
KON-stan-teen pal-ee-OL-uh-gus

Constantine XI became Byzantine emperor in 1449, inheriting a state that was by then little more than the city of Constantinople itself, stripped of nearly every other possession his ancestors had ruled. He spent his four years on the throne pleading with the West for military aid that never arrived in sufficient force, and preparing his capital's ancient defenses as best a nearly empty treasury allowed.

When the Ottoman assault finally broke through on 29 May 1453, tradition holds that Constantine tore the imperial insignia from his own armor and threw himself into the fighting rather than survive the city's fall as a captive. His body was never conclusively identified afterward, which helped give rise to a later legend that an angel had turned him to marble rather than let him die. Whatever the truth of his final moments, his choice to die fighting rather than flee closed, in a single act, the line of Roman emperors that had run unbroken since Augustus.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Giovanni Giustiniani
joh-VAH-nee joos-tee-nee-AH-nee

Giovanni Giustiniani was a Genoese nobleman and professional soldier who arrived in Constantinople in January 1453 with several hundred of his own men, answering Constantine XI's call for help when almost no other Western power responded in force. Constantine placed him in overall command of the city's land defenses, an unusual honor for a foreign volunteer.

Giustiniani's troops anchored the defense of the weakest stretch of wall for seven exhausting weeks. On 29 May, in the fighting's final hours, he was severely wounded and carried from the field to a ship in the harbor. His men's morale broke at almost the exact moment the Kerkoporta gate was found open behind them, and the wall he had defended so long fell soon after. No public-domain portrait of Giustiniani survives; none is known to have been painted in his own lifetime.

📚 PART 2 · THE END OF ROME

Take a moment to register the scale of what ended in 1453. The line of Roman emperors stretched back through Justinian (Week 2), through Constantine's founding of Constantinople in 330, and through Augustus in 27 BC. It had continued in some form for nearly 1,500 years. It ended, finally and completely, with Constantine XI in the streets of his own capital. Mehmet II took the additional title “Caesar of Rome” for himself. He understood exactly what he had accomplished, whatever the West's reluctance to grant an Ottoman sultan that Roman inheritance.

Mehmet II allowed his soldiers three days of plunder, the customary price of a city taken by storm under the era's laws of war. Then he halted the sack himself and began restoring the city he intended to make his own new capital. He resettled merchants and craftsmen from across his empire into the largely emptied city and repaired its markets and aqueducts. Within a generation, the city, soon known more commonly by its Ottoman name, was thriving again as a genuine imperial capital rather than the depopulated shell it had become under its last Roman rulers. A later Greek legend held that Constantine XI had not truly died. An angel, the story went, had turned him to marble and hidden him beneath the city, waiting to return and reclaim it: a wishful tale with obvious appeal to a people who had just lost their last emperor, whatever its evident lack of historical basis.

📚 PART 3 · WHAT MIGHT HAVE BEEN

Before he ever cast a cannon for Mehmet II, the engineer Orban first offered his services to Constantine XI. The emperor wanted the weapon desperately. He could not afford Orban's price. Centuries of shrinking territory and trade had left the Byzantine treasury with no gold to spare. Orban went to the Ottoman court instead, where Mehmet II paid whatever he asked. Had Constantinople's finances held up even a little longer, the single most decisive weapon of the siege might have battered the Ottoman camp instead of the city's own walls. It is one of this lesson's clearest examples of contingency, not inevitability, shaping a historical outcome.

A second contingent failure compounded the first. Ottoman soldiers found it, slipped through, and raised their banner on a tower behind the main defensive line before most defenders even realized the walls had been turned. At almost the same moment, Giovanni Giustiniani, the Genoese commander whose volunteers had anchored the defense for weeks, was severely wounded and carried from the field. His departure broke the defenders' morale at the exact hour the Kerkoporta breach opened behind them. Neither failure alone might have doomed the city. Together, within the same hour, they did.

A Venetian relief fleet did set out to help the city, but it left port too late and was still working its way through the Aegean when Constantinople fell. Had it sailed even two or three weeks earlier, it might have reached the Golden Horn while the defense still held. Constantine XI had appealed repeatedly to the West for help, and the 1439 Council of Florence, which you will read about tomorrow, had promised exactly that kind of support in exchange for religious reunion. Only a modest force of Genoese and Venetian volunteers actually arrived. The broader Western aid the union was meant to secure never reached Constantinople in time, or in strength, a failure tomorrow's lesson explains in full.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

The fall of Constantinople is one of the great hinge-points of world history, closing the ancient world's last living political link to Rome. It is also, as tomorrow's lesson shows, a beginning: the flight of Greek scholars and their books westward, in the years just before and after 1453, would help ignite one of the West's greatest intellectual flowerings. The empire's fall also forces a harder question than simple triumph or tragedy. Mehmet II's swift, deliberate rebuilding of the city he had just conquered is itself a case study in the Classical Republican standards this course applies throughout: a regime's ability to restore order, trade, and settled life after conquest says as much about its character as the conquest itself.

Read more below. was a Florentine diplomat and political theorist writing in the early 16th century. He brought a sharp, unsentimental, empirically-minded eye to Florence's own turbulent political history. His Istorie Fiorentine (History of Florence), which you will read today, recounts the very instability you studied yesterday. A Medici pope, Clement VII, commissioned the work, even though it does not always flatter the family's rivals, or the Medici themselves. It takes the same clear-eyed, virtue-and-vice-attentive approach to political history that later inspired the Classical Republican historiography this course itself draws on. Machiavelli was willing to describe political actors' real motives and failures, rather than idealizing them. That willingness is itself a product of humanism's confidence. Human affairs, humanists believed, could be studied and understood rationally, on their own terms, rather than only through providential or moralizing explanation.

📚 PART 3 · A REPUBLIC IN MARBLE

HumanismHYOO-muh-niz-umThe Renaissance intellectual movement centered on close study of classical Greek and Roman texts as models of eloquence, ethics, and human achievement. See the glossary box.'s confidence in the individual is easiest to see in a single famous commission. In 1501, the city of Florence hired the young sculptor Michelangelo to carve a colossal marble David for its cathedral. The city's government instead placed the finished statue outside its own town hall, the Palazzo Vecchio, as a civic symbol rather than a religious one. David, the underdog shepherd who defeated the giant Goliath, stood for republican Florence itself. The city saw itself as small and exposed, yet defiant against more powerful enemies such as Milan or the Medici's own rivals. A humanist artist's individual genius had produced, quite deliberately, a political statement about the kind of city Florence believed itself to be. Standard W.4.3's demand to name individual contributors is easy to satisfy here. This was a specific commission, by a specific government, to a specific twenty-six-year-old sculptor, not an anonymous guild project.

Michelangelo was a Florentine sculptor, painter, and architect whose individual reputation, even in his own lifetime, matched or exceeded that of the patrons who commissioned his work. A single specific commission, by a specific government, to a specific young sculptor, produced one of the most recognizable political statements in Western art. Michelangelo went on to paint the Sistine Chapel ceiling in Rome and to design part of St. Peter's Basilica, but the Florentine David remains the clearest example of a humanist artist's individual genius put directly to civic use.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

The Renaissance's confidence in human reason, individual achievement, and careful observation of the natural and political world did not stay confined to art and history. As you will see in the coming modules, this same confidence would soon be turned toward religious authority itself. Later still, it would turn toward the natural world in the Scientific Revolution. Notice, too, what humanismHYOO-muh-niz-umThe Renaissance intellectual movement centered on close study of classical Greek and Roman texts as models of eloquence, ethics, and human achievement. See the glossary box. did not do. It did not abandon virtue as the measure of a life well lived. It relocated that measure from purely religious achievement toward a fuller picture of human excellence. Machiavelli's own histories and Michelangelo's own David, in their different ways, still took that fuller measure entirely seriously.

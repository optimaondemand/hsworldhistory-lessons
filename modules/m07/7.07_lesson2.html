📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
By 1400, an empire that had once ruled from Spain to Persia was reduced to a single, magnificent, doomed city.

The Byzantine Empire's final decades were a story of desperate diplomacy against overwhelming odds. Byzantine emperors repeatedly traveled to the West in person, an extraordinary and humbling step, to beg for military aid. Emperor Manuel II Palaiologos spent nearly four years, from 1399 to 1403, touring the courts of Western Europe himself. He visited Paris and even London, where England's King Henry IV hosted him through the winter. He came home, though, with fine words and little real help. His son and successor, John VIII Palaiologos, tried again decades later. John personally attended a great church council in Italy. There, he agreed to reunite the Orthodox and Catholic churches, divided since the Great Schism of 1054 you first met in Week 9, in exchange for promised military aid. Many of the Orthodox clergy and ordinary Byzantines back home never accepted that reunion once John returned. They saw it as a surrender of their own religious tradition, bought too cheaply and for too little in return. You will look more closely at that council next week. Western Europe's response was thin and inconsistent even so. A crusading army was finally raised in 1444 under the Hungarian commander John Hunyadi and King Wladyslaw III of Poland and Hungary. 🛡 BATTLE
Varna, 1444

A crusading army under John Hunyadi and King Wladyslaw III of Poland and Hungary marched into the Balkans in 1444, hoping to relieve growing Ottoman pressure on Constantinople and the wider region before it was too late to matter.

Ottoman forces under Sultan Murad II crushed the crusaders near the Black Sea port of Varna in a decisive, one-sided battle that ended any realistic hope of quick relief. Wladyslaw himself was killed in the fighting, his body reportedly never recovered afterward. No comparable Western expedition followed, and Byzantium's last realistic hope of large-scale rescue effectively died with him on that field, unmourned by any second attempt.

📚 PART 1 · WHY THE WEST DID NOT COME

Why did the West, which had once mobilized tens of thousands of knights for the First Crusade, do so little for Byzantium at its moment of greatest need? Several genuine, overlapping causes deserve weight, rather than a single simple explanation. One was deep and lingering religious distrust between Orthodox and Catholic Christians. That distrust dated to 1054, when a papal legate and the Patriarch of Constantinople had excommunicated one another, and was reinforced by the West's own catastrophic sack of Constantinople in 1204 (Week 9). Crusader looters carried off relics, treasures, and even the famous bronze horses that once stood over the city's great hippodrome, and many of these never returned. Byzantines had good reason to distrust promises of Western help, no matter how urgent their own need became. Another was Western Europe's own preoccupations, including the Hundred Years War you studied last week. A third was simple geographic distance, and the practical difficulty of mounting another large expedition after the costly failure at Varna. No one of these fully explains Byzantium's isolation. Together, though, they do.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Giovanni Giustiniani
joo-stee-NYAH-nee

Giustiniani commanded a private company of several hundred professional soldiers, arriving at Constantinople in early 1453 without any royal government behind him, purely on his own judgment that the cause was genuinely worth his own sword and his men's lives.

Constantine XI's decision to place a foreign volunteer in charge of the land walls' defense reflects both how thin Byzantium's own resources had become and how seriously the emperor took Giustiniani's evident professional skill. No contemporary portrait of Giustiniani is known to survive today, a small reminder of how thin the historical record can be even for figures whose own actions mattered enormously to the outcome.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Constantine XI Palaiologos
KON-stan-teen pal-ee-OL-uh-gus

Constantine XI came to a throne in 1449 that ruled little beyond Constantinople's own walls and a handful of scattered nearby territories, a hollowed-out remnant of the empire his own predecessors had once ruled from Spain all the way to Persia at its height.

Rather than stand on ceremony, he placed the Genoese volunteer Giovanni Giustiniani in command of the city's land defenses, a pragmatic decision that shows a ruler willing to trust proven skill over inherited rank when his own capital's very survival was genuinely at stake, whatever the cost to protocol or precedent among his own remaining courtiers.

📚 PART 3 · A CITY ALONE

Constantinople's recovery after 1204 was itself only partial. Crusader forces from the West had occupied the city and installed a short-lived Latin Empire in its place. The city Michael reclaimed, though, never regained the wealth, population, or military strength it had held before 1204. It was a restored throne sitting atop a hollowed-out empire. Constantinople itself may have held several hundred thousand people at its height under Justinian. By the mid-15th century, its population had shrunk to as few as 50,000 people by some estimates, scattered across a city built to hold vastly more. Whole neighborhoods within the walls had emptied out into farmland and ruins. A traveler crossing the city in these final decades would have passed as many abandoned gardens and fallen-in houses as functioning streets.

By the reign of the last Byzantine emperor, Constantine XI, who came to the throne in 1449, the empire he ruled consisted of little beyond Constantinople's own walls and a handful of nearby territories. Yet Constantinople itself remained one of the most heavily fortified cities in the world. It was protected by the massive Theodosian land wallsThe Theodosian WallsA triple line of moat, outer wall and taller inner wall that no army had broken through by direct assault in a thousand years of standing. Read more below., completed in the 5th century. These walls ran in a triple line: a deep moat, then an outer wall, then a taller inner wall behind it. In a thousand years, no army had ever broken through those land walls by direct assault. Even the disaster of 1204 had come by sea and by political betrayal, not by breaching the walls themselves. Constantine XI would need every one of those walls' advantages. The ruler who would soon move against the city, the young Ottoman sultan Mehmet II, was already assembling the largest siege the city had ever faced, the subject of next week's lesson.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Theodosian Walls

Completed in the 5th century under Theodosius II, the land walls ran roughly four miles across the base of Constantinople's peninsula, backed by a deep moat and an outer wall before reaching the taller, thicker inner wall that formed the final line of defense.

For a thousand years the design worked. Every attacker who took the city, including the crusaders in 1204, did so by sea or by treachery rather than by breaching the land walls directly. By 1453, though, the walls would face a weapon their builders could never have anticipated or designed against: massed, sustained cannon fire from siege guns built specifically to break stone this thick.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

Notice the arc. The empire survived Rome's fall in Week 2. It reconquered the Mediterranean under Justinian. It weathered Justinian's Plague, the Crusades, and centuries of Islamic and Turkic pressure. Even a foreign occupation of its own capital in 1204 did not end it outright. Now, though, it stands nearly alone against the empire that will finally end it. Keep this full thousand-year arc in mind for next week. What falls in 1453 is not a sudden collapse, but the last chapter of a very long, very slow decline. A regime that once solved the first-order political problems this course keeps asking about, internal order and capacity for defense, had by Constantine XI's reign lost the resources to solve either one on its own. That loss, not any single defeat, is the real story of Byzantium's long twilight.

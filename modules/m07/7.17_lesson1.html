📚 PART 1 · BANKING, WOOL, AND THE MEDICI

The city's wool guild, the Arte della Lana, organized production across dozens of specialized workshops, from washing and dyeing to weaving and finishing. It exported finished cloth as far as England and the eastern Mediterranean. The Medici family built one of Europe's largest banking houses by the early 15th century. Its branch offices in Rome, Venice, Bruges, and London handled deposits, currency exchange, and loans to popes and princes alike. They used that fortune to dominate Florentine politics without ever formally abolishing the city's republican constitution. In practice, they ruled through patronage, influence, and strategic marriage rather than outright monarchy. Venice's own economic strength rested on maritime trade rather than wool or banking. Its state-run shipyard, the Arsenal, employed thousands of workers using standardized parts and assembly-line methods centuries before the term existed. In a crisis, the Arsenal could build and fully equip a war galley in as little as a single day. Naples was ruled by the Aragonese crown of Spain. Rome was governed directly by the Pope as both a religious and a temporal ruler. Together these five rounded out the major powers whose shifting alliances defined Italian politics for the next century. Standard W.4.1 asks you to name the specific economic and political causes behind city-states like Florence. One cause was control of profitable trade routes and industries. A second was the absence of a single dominating king able to suppress urban self-government, unlike in France or England by this period. A third was a communal tradition of self-rule stretching back to the chartered towns you studied in Module 6.

📚 PART 3 · A REPUBLIC TESTED

Florence's instability was not only a matter of elite rivalry. In 1378, wool workers known as the Ciompi rose up and briefly seized control of the city. They had no guild of their own and no vote in the city's government, and they demanded representation and better wages. Their government lasted only a few weeks before Florence's wealthier guilds regained control. The revolt exposed how narrow Florence's own republicanism actually was in practice, since it excluded the very laborers whose work built the city's wool wealth. Measured against the Classical Republican standard this course applies, the Ciompi Revolt is a case of a regime failing its own first-order political problem. It failed to bind an entire working population into the common good it claimed to serve.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Cosimo de' Medici
KOH-zee-moh deh MEH-dee-chee

Cosimo de' Medici's rise to power in Florence was never a foregone conclusion. Rival families, fearing his growing wealth and influence, had him exiled from the city in 1433 on trumped-up political charges. He spent a year in exile at Venice, maintaining his banking network and his political alliances from a distance. Florentine politics shifted within that single year; the faction that had exiled him lost its own grip on power, and Florence recalled Cosimo in 1434.

He returned not as a conqueror but as a private citizen, and from that quiet position he built the unofficial, patronage-based dominance this course describes. Had the exile held only a little longer, the whole subsequent history of Medici Florence, and the patronage that funded the early Renaissance, might have looked very different. The most serious later test of his family's power came decades after Cosimo's own death, in 1478, when a rival family conspired with the pope himself to assassinate his grandsons during Mass.

Rather than simply defending the republic that briefly governed Milan after the Visconti line ended, Sforza used the private army loyal to him personally to seize the dukedom for himself in 1450.

His success turned an ordinary hired defender into a founding duke, and his descendants ruled Milan as a hereditary dynasty for generations afterward, displacing the Visconti line entirely. Sforza's career is a cautionary case study in the danger of relying on rented military force: a regime that must pay someone else to defend it risks losing that same capacity for defense to whoever it pays. Machiavelli, whom you will meet later this week, drew directly on cases like Sforza's own when he later argued that a prince who relies on mercenary armies rules on borrowed strength that can turn against him at any time.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

Political fragmentation, commercial wealth, and fierce inter-city competition together explain why the Renaissance began here, in these particular cities, rather than in a large, unified kingdom. But the same evidence tells a second story. The Ciompi Revolt, the Medici's near-collapse in 1433, the Pazzi conspiracy, and Sforza's seizure of Milan all show that fragmentation carried real first-order political costs. Among them: narrow representation, fragile internal peace, and armies whose loyalty could be bought and sold. Tomorrow's lesson turns to what all this wealth and instability actually produced, a genuine intellectual and artistic revolution.

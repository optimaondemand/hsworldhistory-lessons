👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Osman I
OHS-mahn

Osman ruled a small frontier beylikBAY-likTurkish for a small frontier principality, such as Osman I's original Ottoman state. See the glossary box., one of many competing Turkish principalities strung along Byzantium's exposed Anatolian border, distinguished mainly by its favorable position for further raiding and expansion westward into weakening Byzantine territory.

A later Ottoman legend holds that Osman dreamed of a great tree growing from his own body, its branches spreading out to shelter the whole world, a foundation story his successors used to cast their dynasty's rise as destined rather than merely opportunistic.

Osman died around 1323 or 1324, just before his son Orhan captured the city of Bursa and turned his father's small frontier holding into a real, lasting state.

🏯 CITY
Bursa

Bursa's capture in 1326 gave the young Ottoman dynasty its first genuine city and administrative seat, a major upgrade from the scattered frontier encampments that had served Osman's own generation of raiders and warriors on the open steppe frontier.

Even after the capital later moved first to Edirne and finally to Constantinople itself, Bursa retained enormous prestige as the dynasty's ancestral seat, and early sultans continued to build mosques, tombs and religious schools there for generations, long after the city had ceased to actually govern the wider empire it had once proudly ruled from at the young dynasty's own humble beginning.

This detour matters for a course built around contingency. Had Timur not intervened when he did, Constantinople might well have fallen half a century earlier than it actually did. That single battle changed the whole shape of the story you will read tomorrow.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Bayezid I
by-uh-ZEED

Bayezid earned his nickname through the sheer speed of his campaigning, moving his armies between fronts in the Balkans and Anatolia faster than his enemies could react or organize a response, and he besieged Constantinople itself in the 1390s without quite managing to take the city.

His overconfidence proved costly in the end. Provoked into a fight with the far larger army of Timur, Bayezid was decisively defeated and captured at Ankara in 1402. He died in captivity within the year, and the empire he had expanded so quickly collapsed into a full decade of civil war among his own sons.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Timur (Tamerlane)
TEE-moor

Timur built an empire stretching from India to the edge of Anatolia through relentless, often brutal campaigning, styling himself heir to Genghis Khan's Mongol legacy even though he was not himself descended from the Mongol royal line at all.

His decisive victory over Bayezid I at Ankara in 1402 could easily have opened the road to Constantinople for his own forces instead. Timur had no lasting interest in ruling Ottoman territory, though, and marched east again soon afterward, leaving the wreckage of Ottoman authority for Bayezid's own sons to fight over among themselves. He died in 1405 while preparing a further campaign against Ming China.

🛡 BATTLE
Ankara, 1402

Bayezid marched to meet Timur's much larger army near Ankara in July 1402, reportedly with troops already exhausted by a long forced march and thirsty after Timur's forces had seized control of the area's water sources beforehand, a real tactical disadvantage before the fighting even began.

The battle ended in total, humiliating defeat. Bayezid was captured, and the sultanate he had built collapsed into more than a decade of civil war among his own sons. Constantinople, which Bayezid had besieged only years before, gained a reprieve of roughly half a century it could never have won on its own strength alone.

📚 PART 2 · THE JANISSARIES

Central to Ottoman military success, once the sultanate had recovered, was an institution called the devshirmedev-SHEER-mehThe Ottoman system of levying and converting Christian boys from the Balkans, training the most capable into Janissary soldiers or administrators. See the glossary box.. Under this system, the sultan's officials periodically levied a tribute of Christian boys from conquered Balkan territories, typically between about eight and eighteen years old. These boys were converted to Islam. Many were first placed with Turkish farming families for several years, to learn the Turkish language, Islamic religious practice, and rural Ottoman customs before any formal military training began. Only afterward did the most capable move on to specialized training schools, and finally into the elite corps of professional soldiers personally loyal to the sultan alone: the JanissariesThe JanissariesAn elite corps of soldiers raised from conscripted Christian boys, converted and trained from childhood, and loyal to the sultan alone rather than any tribe or noble house. Read more below.. Not every recruit became a soldier. The most promising were instead trained for administration. A number of devshirme-origin officials rose all the way to Grand Viziervih-ZEERThe Ottoman sultan's chief minister, an office some devshirme-raised officials rose all the way to. See the glossary box., the sultan's own chief minister, a striking degree of social mobility for young men taken from their families by force.

Janissaries owed their position entirely to the sultan, not to inherited noble status or tribal loyalty. Because of that, they functioned as a genuinely meritocratic force. They were free of the competing aristocratic loyalties that could weaken a feudal army. This system had real echoes of the Mongol meritocratic promotion you studied last week. Both systems achieved a similar practical result: a disciplined force loyal to the ruler alone. They reached that result through very different, and for the families involved, very differently painful mechanisms. In later decades, Janissary regiments were also among the first infantry in the region to adopt early gunpowder firearms as standard equipment, a technological edge that would matter enormously in the siege you read about next week.

The Janissaries

A Janissary wore a distinctive uniform capped by a tall felt headdress, and carried, in later generations, an early gunpowder firearm rather than the bow and lance more typical of the era's cavalry-heavy armies elsewhere in the wider region.

📚 PART 3 · A SHRINKING EMPIRE

Sultan Murad II took the throne in 1421, not long after the Interregnum ended. He spent his reign steadily rebuilding and extending Ottoman power in the Balkans and Anatolia alike. Ottoman rule, like the earlier Islamic states you studied in Module 2, generally tolerated Christian and Jewish subjects. Those subjects paid a special tax rather than facing forced conversion, even as the empire came to govern many different peoples. That relative tolerance gave the growing Ottoman state a real internal stability. Combined with the Janissary system's meritocratic discipline, it matched the state's military success with genuine staying power.

By the early 15th century, Ottoman territory nearly surrounded what remained of the Byzantine Empire. Byzantium by this point had shrunk to little more than the city of Constantinople itself and a few scattered outposts. This was a shadow of the empire that had reconquered Italy and North Africa under Justinian (Week 2). It was a shadow, too, of the empire that had once stretched deep into Anatolia. Standard W.2.7 asks you to weigh a whole list of causes for Byzantine decline together. Justinian's Plague was one. Centuries of external pressure were another. So was the Crusades' devastating 1204 sack of Constantinople itself (Week 9). Sustained internal political weakness, compounding continuous Ottoman pressure, was a fourth. This is not a single cause but an accumulation across nearly a thousand years. That is exactly the kind of layered, contingent causation this course has asked you to trace since Module 1.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

Byzantium's pleas for Western military help went largely unanswered this time, much like Alexios I's appeal that helped launch the First Crusade three centuries earlier. Any help that did come arrived too late and in too small a force, as tomorrow's lesson on the empire's final years will show. Notice, too, how the Timurid detour you read about today delayed the ending by decades without ever changing its direction. Contingency can postpone an outcome. It does not always prevent one. Keep both lessons in mind together. A state's internal recovery, like the Ottomans' after 1413, can run on a very different clock than an old empire's inability to recover, like Byzantium's after 1204. Even so, both clocks were heading toward the same eventual result.

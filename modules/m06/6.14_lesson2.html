📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In the span of a single century, Western Europe endured a catastrophic famine, the deadliest epidemic in its recorded history, a schism that gave Christendom three rival popes at once, and a war that lasted over a hundred years.

The growth you studied yesterday had limits. Beginning in 1315, several years of unusually cold, wet weather across Northern Europe ruined harvests repeatedly. Some historians connect this shift to the start of a longer cooling period sometimes called the Little Ice Age, though the exact causes remain debated. The bad weather produced the Great FamineGRAYT FAM-inThe 1315–1317 famine caused by years of ruined harvests, which killed an estimated 10–15% of the population in the hardest-hit regions. See the glossary box. of 1315–1317, which killed perhaps 10–15% of the population in the hardest-hit regions. A cattle disease then swept through Europe's herds in 1319 and 1320, on top of the ruined harvests, striking already weakened farms even harder. Survivors of both disasters were left seriously weakened well before an even greater catastrophe arrived a generation later.

The plague's demographic catastrophe had a paradoxical economic effect. Roughly a third of the workforce was gone, so surviving laborers and peasants found their labor suddenly far more valuable. Many were able to negotiate better terms, higher wages, or even outright freedom from serfdom. This accelerated the slow decline of the manorial system you studied in Module 3. In England, wages for some laborers roughly doubled within a few years of the outbreak. Parliament tried to legislate the shift away in 1351 with the Statute of Labourers, capping wages at pre-plague levels by law. The statute mostly failed. Simple demand for scarce labor proved far stronger than any law written against it. Crisis and opportunity, this course keeps reminding you, are not always opposites.

🏯 CITY
Florence

Already a major banking and textile center by 1347, Florence was among the largest and wealthiest cities in Western Europe when the plague reached it, its dense, crowded streets giving the disease an unusually efficient path from house to house and family to family.

Boccaccio's account describes a city where ordinary social bonds broke down entirely under the strain, with the dead sometimes left unburied for lack of anyone willing to handle the bodies, even as the wealth and learning that had made Florence great endured and eventually helped fuel its later Renaissance flowering a century afterward, once the city's population and trade had recovered.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Giovanni Boccaccio
boh-KAH-choh

Boccaccio lived through the 1348 outbreak in Florence and drew directly on what he had personally witnessed there, describing abandoned households, hastily dug mass graves, and the collapse of ordinary social bonds under the strain of so much sudden death.

His Decameron frames a hundred stories around ten young Florentines who flee the stricken city for the safety of the countryside, entertaining one another with tales while the plague rages behind them, a literary structure that turned his own eyewitness horror into one of the founding works of Italian prose literature and a lasting model for later European storytelling to build upon.

👥 PEOPLES
The Flagellants

Bands of flagellants marched from town to town across parts of Northern Europe in 1349, dressed in white robes marked with a red cross, publicly whipping their own bare backs in rhythmic processions meant to atone for a sin they sincerely believed had brought the plague upon all of Christendom.

Their movement drew large, emotionally charged crowds wherever it passed and, in places, spilled over into violence against Jewish communities wrongly blamed for the plague. Church authorities eventually condemned the flagellants as dangerously unorthodox and moved to suppress the movement outright, but not before it had spread across much of the region unchecked.

🛡 BATTLE
Agincourt, 1415

Henry V's exhausted, heavily outnumbered army took up a narrow position squeezed between two patches of woodland, where French numbers could never be brought fully to bear, and simply waited for the larger French force to attack across the open field.

Heavy autumn rain had turned the freshly plowed field to deep, clinging mud. French knights advancing on foot in full armor grew exhausted long before ever reaching the English line, while English longbowmen loosed their arrows far faster than French crossbowmen could reload their own weapons. Terrain and tactics, not raw numbers, decided the day, exactly as this course keeps asking you to notice.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Joan of Arc
JOHN of ARK

Joan was an illiterate peasant girl from Domremy who claimed to hear the voices of saints instructing her to help the French royal cause, an extraordinary claim that nonetheless won her an audience with the uncrowned King Charles VII.

Given a place with the army besieging Orleans, she helped rally demoralized French troops to lift the siege in 1429, a turning point after decades of English advantage. Captured the following year and handed to English-allied authorities, she was tried for heresy and burned at the stake in 1431, only to be posthumously rehabilitated and later canonized, her brief career one of the war's sharpest reminders that a single person's conviction can redirect a nation's fortunes.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

Famine, plague, schism, and war together mark a genuine turning point. The confident, expanding High Middle Ages you studied yesterday gave way to a shaken, transformed Late Middle Ages. None of these four crises caused the others in any simple, direct way. Each instead weakened a different pillar of medieval order at nearly the same moment: royal authority, church authority, and the manorial economy alike. Western Europe was genuinely changed by 1400, in ways it would carry into the Renaissance and Reformation you will study in the coming modules.

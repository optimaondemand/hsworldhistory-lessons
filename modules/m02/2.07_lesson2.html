👥 PEOPLES
The Franks
FRANKS

The Franks differed from the Goths and Vandals in one decisive way. Those peoples migrated hundreds of miles and had to build kingdoms among strangers, cut off from where they came from. The Franks simply pushed outward from the Rhineland they already held, so their new territory always backed onto their old one.

They were known for their weapons. The illustration beside this box shows Frankish warriors in mail with the throwing axe the Romans called the francisca, which a rank of men could hurl together to break a shield wall just before contact. They also wore their hair long, and among the Merovingians long hair was the mark of royal blood. To cut a prince's hair was to remove him from the succession.

📚 PART 1 · CLOVIS'S CONQUESTS

Rome's rule in northern Gaul did not end in 476, and it did not end with any single, famous barbarian king. In the most literal sense, it ended with Clovis. A Roman military commander named Syagrius had held on to an independent state around SoissonsSWAH-sonnThe town north-east of Paris that was the seat of Syagrius, the last Roman ruler in Gaul. Clovis took it in 486, and the story of the looted vase happened just afterwards. Find it on the map. for a full decade after the Western Empire's official collapse. He even called himself “King of the Romans,” though no emperor remained to grant him that title. In 486, Clovis defeated Syagrius in battle and took his territory. It was the last piece of Gaul still governed in Rome's name.

Gregory of ToursGREG-uh-ree of TOORThe bishop whose History of the Franks is very nearly the only narrative source for this whole century. Read more below., whose History of the Franks you are reading this week, preserves a vivid story from right after that victory. Clovis's soldiers had looted a precious vase from a church near Soissons. Clovis hoped to please the local bishop, so he asked his men to let him take the vase as an extra share of his own portion of the plunder. One soldier refused. He smashed the vase with his axe and shouted that Clovis would get only his fair share like everyone else. Clovis said nothing at the time. But a year later, at a review of his troops, he walked up to that same soldier and criticized his poorly kept weapons. He threw the man's own axe to the ground, and as the soldier bent to pick it up, Clovis split his skull open. “Thus you did to the vase at Soissons,” he said. The story is about more than religion. It shows you Frankish kingship: personal honor, and a king's justice delayed but never forgotten. This is the same king you are about to meet as a pious convert, still ruling exactly as a Germanic war-leader was expected to.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Clovis I
KLOH-vis

Clovis became king of one Frankish group at about fifteen and spent thirty years absorbing the others, sometimes by war and sometimes by killing rival kinsmen. Gregory of Tours reports that he complained late in life of having no relatives left, and adds drily that Clovis said it hoping to flush out any he had missed.

The illumination beside this box shows the moment that mattered most. He stands in the font at Reims while Remigius baptises him and a dove brings down the holy oil, a detail added by later writers. What is not legend is the consequence. His baptism put him on the same side as the bishops and the Gallo-Roman population he ruled, at the exact moment every neighbouring Germanic king was on the other side of that line.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Gregory of Tours
GREG-uh-ree of TOOR

Gregory became bishop of Tours in 573, about sixty years after Clovis died, and wrote his history while dealing with Clovis's quarrelsome grandsons. The illumination beside this box shows him confronting one of them, King Chilperic, which is a fair picture of his working life.

Read him carefully. He is a bishop writing to show that Catholic kings prosper and others do not, so his Clovis is a second Constantine and his enemies come to bad ends. But he also records the vase of Soissons, the axe in the skull, and the murders of relatives, without softening any of it. A writer with a case to make who still reports what damages it is a writer worth trusting on the facts.

Most other Germanic kingdoms of the era (the Visigoths in Spain, the Ostrogoths in Italy, the Vandals in Africa) had adopted Arian Christianity. This doctrine denied that Christ the Son was fully equal to God the Father, and the Gallo-Roman population overwhelmingly rejected it as heresy. According to tradition, Clovis's Catholic wife Clotilde urged him to convert for years. Clovis himself credited a decisive battlefield victory over a rival Germanic people, the Alamanni, to prayers made to her God. 🛡 BATTLE
Vouille, 507
voo-YAY

Clovis crossed the Loire and met Alaric II somewhere near Poitiers in the spring of 507. Gregory of Tours says the Visigoths fought in their usual way, at a distance with javelins, while the Franks closed to hand strokes, and that the line broke once they did.

Clovis is said to have killed Alaric himself, which is what the miniature beside this box shows. Scholarship cannot fix the battlefield with confidence, and the site is still argued over. The result is not in doubt. Visigothic rule in Gaul ended, the court withdrew to Spain, and the boundary between what would become France and what would become Spain settled roughly where it still runs.

📚 PART 3 · THE MEROVINGIAN INHERITANCE

Clovis was also a lawgiver. Around the time of his conversion, he had the customary law of the Salian Franks written down as the Lex SalicaLEKS SAL-ih-kuhThe written law of the Salian Franks, which put customary rules into a fixed text a king's officers could apply. Read more below., or Salic Law. It set fines and procedures for settling disputes, not unlike the Kentish law code Aethelberht would issue in England roughly a century later. These two Germanic successor kingdoms had little direct contact with each other. Yet each arrived independently at the same basic solution to the same political problem. A king who wants his rule to outlast his own presence in a room needs a common law his subjects can appeal to, not just his own word.

Clovis's descendants, the Merovingian dynasty, ruled Francia for over two centuries. Later kings, though, increasingly left real power to their chief officials, the Mayors of the PalaceMAY-er uv thuh PAL-issThe chief official of the Merovingian Frankish court, who over generations came to hold the kingdom's real power while the king kept only ceremonial authority. See the glossary box.. A legitimate royal bloodline kept ceremonial authority, while an official class actually governed. This arrangement is itself a case study in the Classical Republican standard of judgment: a state whose formal offices no longer match its actual power risks a crisis of legitimacy. That crisis came to a head in the following century, when a Mayor of the Palace's family, the Carolingians, simply took the crown for themselves. That is the subject of a lesson later this quarter.

For now, notice the wider pattern developing across Western Europe by 600. Distinct, Christianized Germanic successor kingdoms (Anglo-Saxon England, Frankish Gaul, Visigothic Spain) were each fusing Roman administrative habits, Germanic custom and kinship structures, and the Christian church into something genuinely new. This is exactly the synthesis of Greco-Roman, Judeo-Christian, and northern European elements the standards ask you to trace. It is happening in parallel, with real variation, across the former Western Empire.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Lex Salica
LEKS SAL-ih-kuh

The Lex SalicaLEKS SAL-ih-kuhLatin for “Salic Law,” the written law code of the Salian Franks that Clovis had recorded. See the glossary box. is mostly a tariff. It lists offences and the payment owed for each: so much for a stolen pig, more for a stolen plough ox, a great deal for killing a free Frank, and more again for killing one in the king's service. Setting the price in advance is the whole point. A family with a fixed sum to claim has less reason to start a feud.

The page beside this box was copied around 794, nearly three centuries after Clovis, which tells you the text stayed in use. One clause about inheriting family land was still being cited by French lawyers a thousand years later to argue that a woman could not inherit the throne.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

Clovis's religious choice in 496 is a textbook case of a contingent decision with enormous downstream consequences. It aligned the most successful Germanic kingdom with the majority religion of the population it ruled, at the exact moment its ArianAIR-ee-unA Christian teaching, condemned as heresy at the Council of Nicaea in 325, holding that Christ the Son was created by and therefore ranks below God the Father. He could have stayed pagan, or adopted the Arian Christianity most other Germanic kings around him had chosen.

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Rome left Britain in 410. What filled the vacuum was not one kingdom but seven, fighting, converting, and slowly becoming something we would recognize as England.

The legions withdrew from Britain around 410, the same year Alaric sacked Rome. Romano-British society was left to defend itself against raiders. Soon there were settlers too: the Angles, Saxons, and JutesANG-gulz, SAK-sunz and JOOTSThree Germanic peoples from the North Sea coast whose settlement in Britain gave England its name and its language. Over the following two centuries they pushed Romano-British and Celtic populations into the western and northern fringes of the island. These were the ancestors of the Welsh, Cornish, and some Scots. Meanwhile the newcomers carved out a patchwork of small Anglo-Saxon kingdoms across the lowlands.

The British monk Gildas, writing around a century later in a work called On the Ruin of Britain, blamed his own people's moral failures for what happened next. He describes a shortsighted British ruler he calls a “proud tyrant,” later tradition names him Vortigern, who invited Saxon warbands to settle in Britain as paid defenders against raiding Picts and Scots. These warbands were led by men remembered as Hengist and Horsa. According to this tradition, the Saxon mercenaries soon judged their hosts weak. They turned on them, and sent word home for more of their kinsmen to come and take the land outright. Gildas's account is moralizing and almost certainly simplified. But notice the underlying pattern: a Roman or post-Roman authority invites Germanic warriors in as paid soldiers, then loses control of them. That should sound familiar. It is nearly the same mistake Rome itself made with the Goths at the Danube in 376. It is worth asking whether that repetition is coincidence, a shared flaw in how late Roman societies handled outside soldiers, or simply the easiest story for a moralizing writer like Gildas to tell.

👥 PEOPLES
The Angles, Saxons and Jutes
ANG-gulz, SAK-sunz and JOOTS

Bede names three peoples: Angles from the neck of Denmark, Saxons from the German coast, and Jutes from Jutland. The division is neater in his account than it was on the ground, but the general picture holds.

The helmet beside this box was buried in a ship at Sutton Hoo in East Anglia around 625, and it tells you what these people were like better than any chronicle can. It has a face mask with garnet-set eyebrows, and it was made by craftsmen who could work gold to a standard nobody in Britain had matched for two centuries. The same ship held Byzantine silver and coins from Frankish Gaul. These were not isolated raiders. They were a warrior aristocracy plugged into the trade of a wide world.

📚 PART 1 · THE HEPTARCHY

By the 7th century, this patchwork had consolidated into roughly seven principal kingdoms: Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Essex, Kent, Sussex, and Wessex. Historians later nicknamed this period the HeptarchyHEP-tar-keeHistorians' nickname, meaning “rule of seven,” for the seven rival Anglo-Saxon kingdoms that divided England before it was united. Supremacy passed back and forth between rival kingdoms for centuries, decided as often by battle and marriage alliance as by any settled rule of succession. Whichever king held the upper hand at a given moment was sometimes given the title BretwaldaBRET-wahl-duhOld English for “wide-ruler,” an honorary title for whichever Anglo-Saxon king held the upper hand at a given moment, not a real unified crown. See the glossary box., meaning “wide-ruler” or “Britain-ruler.” This was an honorary overlordship, not a real unified crown, and the title passed between Kent, Northumbria, and Mercia as their fortunes rose and fell. There was no England yet. There were only English-speaking peoples who did not yet think of themselves as one nation.

Archaeologists and historians still debate exactly how thoroughly the newcomers replaced, rather than blended with, the Romano-British population they displaced. Older accounts pictured near-total ethnic replacement across the lowlands. More recent archaeological and genetic evidence suggests a messier mix of conquest, displacement, and intermarriage that varied region by region. Keep both possibilities in view rather than assuming the simpler story is the true one.

What began knitting these rival kingdoms into a shared civilization was not politics but religion. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Augustine of Canterbury
aw-GUS-tin

Augustine was prior of a monastery in Rome when Gregory the Great chose him to lead a mission to the Anglo-Saxons. The journey nearly collapsed before it began. Somewhere in Gaul his companions lost their nerve at what they heard about the people ahead of them, and Augustine turned back toward Rome to ask permission to abandon the whole plan. Gregory refused and sent him on.

He landed in Kent in 597 and became the first archbishop of Canterbury. Gregory's written instructions to him were strikingly flexible. Rather than destroy the old shrines, convert them to Christian use, and let converts keep feasts they already knew. Augustine had far less success with the British bishops to the west, who kept their own reckoning of Easter and refused his authority. That unsettled argument is the one Whitby would finally decide.

He had already married a Christian Frankish princess, Bertha, and had let her keep her faith and her chaplain, so when the mission from Rome landed in 597 it was not arriving among strangers.

He heard the missionaries in the open air, reportedly because he feared magic under a roof, and converted along with his court. His more lasting act was the law code he issued afterwards: about ninety short clauses setting a fixed payment for each injury, from a killing down to a chipped fingernail. It is the earliest surviving document written in English, and the earliest in any Germanic language.

📚 PART 2 · LAW AND A DIVIDED CHURCH

Christianity brought more than belief. It brought writing put to a new purpose. Soon after his conversion, Aethelberht of Kent issued the earliest surviving law code written in any Germanic vernacularver-NAK-yoo-lerThe everyday language ordinary people actually spoke and wrote, as opposed to Latin, the church and scholarly language. It was a list of set fines for offenses from theft to injury, meant to replace private blood feud with a predictable, publicly known scale of justice. A king who can promise his people a common, written law they can actually read is solving exactly the political problem this course keeps asking you to notice. How does a society resolve conflicts between its own citizens without tearing itself apart?

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The monastic book
muh-NAS-tik BUUK

A monastery was a workshop as much as a house of prayer. Making one book meant killing a flock of sheep or calves for parchment, scraping and stretching the skins, cutting quills, grinding pigment, and then a year or more of a trained hand copying. The page beside this box comes from a Bible made at Jarrow, Bede's own monastery, around 700. It consumed the hides of roughly fifteen hundred animals.

This is the technology underneath everything else in the lesson. Writing let Aethelberht fix his fines in a form his subjects could check, let the church keep one calendar across seven kingdoms, and let Bede gather documents from across Britain into a single history.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Bede
BEED

Bede was given to the monastery at Jarrow at the age of seven and stayed there for the rest of his life, dying in 735. He almost never travelled. Nearly everything he knew about the wider island came from letters, and from documents he asked correspondents to copy out and send him, which he then named in his text so that a reader could weigh them.

That habit of citing his evidence is why historians still use him. He finished the Ecclesiastical History of the English People in 731, and it is the reason we can write about this period at all. He also did more than anyone to spread the practice of counting years from the birth of Christ, which is why the dates in this lesson are numbered the way they are.

💪 WHY IT MATTERS

Bede calling scattered, warring kingdoms “the English people” is a reminder that a nation's identity and its political unification are two different processes. One can run far ahead of the other. The same pattern shows up in miniature in Aethelberht's law code and in the Whitby decision. A shared law and a shared church calendar are both acts of unification, and both came well before a single crown. Keep this distinction in mind. You will meet it again when the Vikings, and later the Normans, force the political unification Bede's word had already imagined.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Muhammad
moo-HAM-ad

Muhammad's early life gave little hint of what followed. Orphaned young and raised first by his grandfather and then his uncle, he made his living in Mecca's caravan trade and earned a reputation for honesty so strong that people called him al-Amin, the trustworthy one. He married late, well, and by all accounts happily, to a widow who ran the caravans he worked for.

The illustration beside this box shows a scene later Islamic tradition told about him: settling a dispute over the sacred Black Stone by having every clan lift it together on a cloth, sharing the honor rather than letting one clan claim it. Muslim artistic tradition generally avoids depicting Muhammad's face directly, so many historical images, including this one, show him veiled or leave his features unshown out of religious respect.

📚 PART 1 · A MERCHANT'S CALLING

Muhammad's own life before his revelations was already unusual. His father died before he was born, and his mother died when he was six. His grandfather raised him for two years. Then his uncle Abu Talib took him in. Muhammad grew up working in the caravan trade that made Mecca wealthy. He earned a reputation for honesty so strong that people called him al-Amin, “the trustworthy one.”

That reputation changed his life. A wealthy widow named Khadijahkah-DEE-juhThe Meccan businesswoman who hired, married, and became the first convert of the man who worked for her. Read more below. hired him to manage her trading caravans. She was about fifteen years older than Muhammad. Impressed by his character, she proposed marriage, and he accepted. Khadijah became his first convert once the revelations began around 610. Her wealth and standing in Mecca gave him real protection in the movement's early years. Powerful Meccan clans still felt threatened by his message. Mecca's economy depended partly on pilgrims visiting the KaabaKAH-buhThe cube-shaped shrine at Mecca, a pilgrimage site for many gods before Muhammad emptied it of idols in 630. See the glossary box.'s many shrines to different gods. Muhammad's strict monotheism put that income at risk. Opponents boycotted his own clan, Banu Hashim, for years. Then, in 619, Khadijah and Abu Talib both died within months of each other. Muhammad's Meccan opponents grew bolder now that his two protectors were gone. Muslims later called 619 the “Year of Sorrow.” It left Muhammad searching for a safer home for his small community. That search ended three years later at Yathrib.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Khadijah
kah-DEE-juh

Khadijah ran her own trading caravans in a city where that was unusual for a woman, and she hired Muhammad on the strength of his reputation rather than any family connection. She was roughly forty when she proposed marriage to a man about fifteen years younger, and by most accounts the marriage was a close and happy one that lasted until her death.

Her importance to the movement's survival is easy to understate. She was Muhammad's first convert, and her wealth and standing gave him real cover while Mecca's ruling clans were still deciding whether his preaching was a nuisance or a threat. Her death in 619, alongside his uncle's in the same year, removed that cover at almost the worst possible moment, and the persecution that followed is part of what pushed Muhammad toward the HijraHIJ-ruhArabic for Muhammad's 622 migration from Mecca to Medina, which marks year one of the Islamic calendar. 🛡 BATTLE
Badr, 624
BAHDR

A Muslim raiding party set out to intercept a rich Meccan caravan near the wells of Badr, only to find that Mecca had sent an army several times the size of their own force to protect it. Muhammad positioned his men near the wells, denying the larger Meccan force easy water in the desert heat.

The Meccans broke and fled after losing several of their leading men in the fighting. For the small, still-vulnerable Muslim community at Medina, the victory was proof that God favored their cause, and it is remembered in the Qur'ankuh-RAHNThe collection of revelations Muslims hold as scripture. See the glossary box. itself as a sign of that favor.

🛡 BATTLE
Uhud, 625
oo-HOOD

Muhammad posted a company of archers on a low hill beside the battlefield with strict orders to hold their position no matter what happened below. When the Meccan line broke and the Muslims began looting the abandoned camp, most of the archers left the hill to join them.

Meccan cavalry under Khalid ibn al-Walid, who would later become a celebrated Muslim general himself, swept around the now-undefended hill and struck the Muslims from behind. Muhammad was wounded and briefly rumored dead. The battle ended as a costly draw rather than the clear Meccan victory it might have been, and Muslim tradition has treated the archers' broken discipline as the lesson's whole point ever since.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Trench (Khandaq), 627
khan-DAHK

Medina's north side lay open to attack, while palm groves and buildings already shielded its other approaches. Facing a large coalition army, the Muslims dug a broad ditch across that one exposed stretch over several days, a tactic said to have been suggested by Salman, a Persian convert familiar with Sasanian siege defenses.

The trench denied the coalition's cavalry any clean charge into the city. Weeks of stalemate, bad weather, and mutual distrust among the besieging tribes eventually broke the siege without a single decisive battle being fought. It was a modest engineering fix to a specific problem, not a grand strategy, and it worked exactly as intended.

📚 PART 4 · ONE GOD, THREE FAITHS

Islam did not emerge in a religious vacuum. It shares with Judaism and Christianity a common commitment to strict monotheism and a common belief in a final judgment. It also shares a reverence for many of the same figures. These include Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, whom Islam honors as a major prophet, though not as divine. The Qur'ankuh-RAHNThe collection of revelations Muslims hold as scripture. See the glossary box. explicitly identifies Jews and Christians as “People of the Book,” recipients of genuine earlier revelation. It prescribes them a distinct, though subordinate, legal status (dhimmiDIM-eeArabic for the protected legal standing of Jews and Christians under Muslim rule: protected, but not equal, and subject to a special tax rather than forced conversion. See the glossary box.) under Muslim rule rather than forced conversion.

The real disagreements are just as important to understand accurately as the agreements. Christianity holds Jesus to be the divine Son of God, crucified and resurrected. Islam denies his divinity and his crucifixion. It honors him instead as a prophet who foretold Muhammad. Judaism and Islam share a stricter, non-Trinitarian monotheism, along with dietary and legal traditions that have real structural similarities. But they differ on which prophet and revelation is final. Comparing these traditions honestly means neither flattening real disagreements into “we all really believe the same thing,” nor treating the traditions as having nothing whatsoever in common.
